Valuing an Inherited House Without Starting a Family Feud

Every buyout stands or falls on one number. How that number is reached — appraisal, comparable sales, or a negotiated figure — decides whether the family settles or splits.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 2, 2026 · 8 min read

The number is the negotiation. Every probate buyout, every co-owner settlement, every family argument about a house comes down to one figure: what is this house worth, and will every heir believe it? Get that number wrong — or get it right but explain it badly — and the buyout fails. Get it right and show the work, and even heirs who wanted different outcomes can usually accept the same price.

Start with what the number has to do. It has to settle shares, not just describe a house. A one-third heir in a house valued at $300,000 is owed $100,000 before mortgages, debts, and administration costs are accounted for; in a house valued at $360,000, the same heir is owed $120,000. A 20% swing in the valuation is a 20% swing in every heir's payout, which is why valuation disputes feel personal even when they are arithmetic. The first job of any valuation is therefore not precision to the dollar. It is acceptance by every person whose signature the buyout needs.

The most defensible route is usually a professional appraisal. A residential appraisal for a straightforward single-family home typically costs $300 to $600, with complex or high-value properties costing more — a small price for a number that carries a licensed appraiser's name, method, and comparable sales behind it. An appraisal also gives the buyout something a family argument cannot: a document. When an heir asks why the house is worth what the buyout says, the answer is a report with adjustments for condition, size, location, and recent sales, not a sibling's opinion. Where the estate is in probate, that report often serves double duty, supporting both the estate's inventory and the buyout price.

Comparable sales are the second route, and they are the one most families understand intuitively. The question is always which sales count: recent, nearby, similar in size and condition, and genuinely comparable rather than merely nearby. A renovated house two streets over is not a comparable for a house with a twenty-year-old roof and original systems, and a distressed sale next door is not a comparable for a well-kept home. Investors know this gap well: in the second quarter of 2025, the typical investor purchase was $287,000 against a national median sale price of $372,000 in the same quarter — a reminder that the price an investor pays for a house needing work is not the price a family should use to value a house in good repair, or vice versa. The valuation method has to match the house's condition, or the number will be attacked from whichever side it disadvantages.

Condition is where inherited houses most often surprise their heirs. A house lived in for decades by an older owner may carry years of deferred maintenance — roof, systems, accessibility, and code items that a buyer will price in whether the family does or not. Older sellers, research reported in 2026 found, more often list homes described as needing work, and receive lower returns than younger sellers. That is not a judgment on the owner; it is the market pricing the work the next owner must do. A buyout valuation that ignores condition overpays the departing heirs and strands the keeper with the repair bill; a valuation that double-counts condition underpays them and kills the deal. The honest approach is to price the house as it stands, with the repair scope written down and costed, so every heir sees the same deductions.

Costs around the house matter too, because a buyout share is a net figure, not a gross one. Probate itself generally runs 3% to 8% of an estate's total value, with attorney fees and personal-representative compensation making up the largest share; for a $500,000 estate that is roughly $15,000 to $40,000 before beneficiaries receive anything. In states with statutory fee schedules, the percentages are fixed by formula — one well-known schedule charges 4% on the first $100,000, 3% on the next $100,000, 2% on the next $800,000, and 1% on the next $9,000,000 — and those percentages apply to the gross estate, before mortgages and debts are subtracted. A buyout that quotes gross value without showing debts, costs, and the mortgage will feel like a broken promise when the net checks arrive.

Where co-ownership is tangled, valuation carries a second burden: it sets the price of a forced exit as well as a voluntary one. When multiple family members inherit without a will, each holds an undivided interest, and the number of co-owners can grow across generations. Research on heirs' property puts the assessed value of identified properties at approximately $32.3 billion, and notes that figure is conservative. In states that have adopted a uniform partition law — 22 states plus the District of Columbia and the U.S. Virgin Islands as of a 2024 professional update — a co-owner who files for partition triggers a buyout right for the others, priced on an independent appraisal of fair market value, with an open-market sale preferred if a sale is ordered. The appraisal, in that regime, is not just evidence. It is the mechanism.

The practical method, then, is boring in the best way: commission one appraisal every heir can read, or agree in writing on a comparable-sales method before any number is produced; list debts, liens, and the mortgage against the gross value; cost the visible repairs once, in writing; and state the net share each heir receives. Families do not feud over arithmetic they can follow. They feud over numbers that arrive without explanation, from a source they did not choose, at a moment when trust is already thin. A buyout business that makes valuation its most transparent step — published method, shared report, net figures, no surprises — removes the single most common reason these deals collapse.
