The Probate Investor's Guide: Timelines, Costs, and Cash Sales

Probate is slow, it costs a measurable share of the estate, and cash buyers are a record share of the market. An investor who understands all three buys better.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 2, 2026 · 8 min read

Probate rewards patience and punishes surprise. An investor who buys inherited houses needs to know three things before making an offer: how long the estate will take, what it will cost, and who else is bidding. All three have published answers, and all three answers are less comfortable than a sales pitch would suggest. That discomfort is the opportunity: the investor who plans for a slow, costly, cash-heavy process buys houses others abandon halfway through it.

Start with time. The national bar association estimates probate takes around 6 to 9 months. Other surveys put the figure closer to 18 months, and one executor-focused guide reports its own average at about 13 months. Those are not contradictions; they are different estates. A small estate with a clear will and cooperative heirs can move in one to two months. An estate with no will adds months. An estate with many or large debts adds months. An estate with more than ten beneficiaries adds months. A dispute over distribution adds six months to two years on its own. Real estate inside the estate adds its own friction, because a house cannot be distributed until it is valued, maintained, insured, and — if it is to be sold — marketed and closed. An investor who underwrites a probate purchase on a 30-day flip timeline is underwriting a different asset class.

Then cost. Probate generally runs 3% to 8% of an estate's total value, with attorney fees and personal-representative compensation as the largest share. For a $500,000 estate, that is roughly $15,000 to $40,000 before beneficiaries receive anything. Attorney fees alone commonly run 3% to 7% of the estate's value in guides to probate administration, or $150 to $500 per hour where hourly billing applies, with flat fees for straightforward estates roughly $1,500 to $7,000. Court filing fees, appraisals at typically $300 to $600 for a straightforward home, surety bonds, publication notices, and months of taxes, insurance, and maintenance on an empty house all come out of the estate before heirs are paid. An offer that ignores those deductions is not a good offer; it is an offer that will be renegotiated when the net figures appear.

The demand side, meanwhile, is larger than most investors assume. Wealth projected to transfer through 2045 totals $84.4 trillion, with $72.6 trillion going directly to heirs. Home equity held by homeowners age 62 and older reached a record $14.66 trillion in the third quarter of 2025, up 1.9% in the quarter, on an index reading of 511.99 — the highest since the index launched in 2000. And inherited-home transfers are already visible in deed records: about 340,000 in the year ending August 2025, or 7% of all transfers seen, the highest share the property-data firm has published. The pipeline of inherited houses is not a forecast. It is already flowing, and it widens every year as the generation holding that equity passes its houses on.

Competition for those houses is specific, not general. Investors bought 10.8% of homes purchased in the second quarter of 2025, up from 10.7% a year earlier but below the 12.1% peak of the second quarter of 2022. Small investors accounted for 62.5% of investor purchases in that quarter, the second-highest small-investor share on record. The typical investor purchase was $287,000, more than $80,000 below the national median sale price of $372,000 in the same quarter. Read together, those figures describe a market dominated not by institutions buying trophy assets, but by small buyers purchasing lower-priced, often work-needing houses — exactly the profile of many inherited homes. The probate investor's edge is therefore rarely price alone. It is certainty: cash, no financing contingency, and a close that does not depend on a lender approving a house with tangled title or deferred maintenance.

Cash is the other half of the market, and it is at a record level. All-cash purchases averaged 26% of transactions over the most recent year surveyed by the national realtors' association — an all-time high, against fewer than one in ten buyers paying all cash between 2003 and 2010 — and nearly one in three repeat buyers paid all cash. For a probate seller, cash removes the two risks that most often kill an estate sale: a lender's appraisal coming in below the agreed price, and a buyer's financing collapsing during probate's long timeline. For the investor, cash is a discount mechanism, but it should be priced honestly: heirs who accept a cash discount in exchange for speed and certainty are making a rational trade, and a buyer who presents it as anything else will lose the next deal to a competitor who tells the truth about it.

The risks that remain are title, condition, and family dynamics, in that order. Title must be clear before a sale can close, and clearing it — confirming every heir, recording every transfer, resolving any lien or dispute — is the step most likely to delay a probate purchase. Condition must be priced from inspection, not from photographs, because inherited houses often carry deferred maintenance that only a walk-through reveals. And family dynamics must be treated as a closing condition, not a soft factor: a buyout or estate sale needs the signatures of the people authorized to give them, and an heir who feels underpaid can delay a closing longer than any court backlog. The investor's checklist, then, is short: verify authority, verify title, inspect the house, price the net — not the gross — and offer cash with a timetable the estate can actually meet.

Put together, the probate investor's guide is not a trick. It is a calendar, a cost schedule, and a cash position. Probate takes 6 to 9 months at the straightforward end and far longer when contested. It costs a single-digit percentage of the estate, plus months of carrying costs on the house. The supply of inherited houses is large, documented, and growing. The buyers competing for them are mostly small, mostly cash, and mostly buying below the median price. An investor who enters that market with patience, verified title, and honest net pricing does not need to outbid anyone. The estate will choose the buyer who can actually close — and close without reopening the family's argument.
