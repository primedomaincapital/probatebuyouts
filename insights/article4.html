The Sibling Who Won’t Leave

Every sibling owns a share. One sibling lives in the house. Nobody can evict a co-owner — and that standoff is the probate buyout’s real market.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

Three siblings inherit a house. One of them lives in it. The other two want their share of the money. Nobody can evict the one who lives there, because all three own it. That sentence is the whole probate buyout market: a financing problem wrapped in a family problem, where the legal reality is that a co-owner cannot be removed from a house they partly own, and the practical reality is that the house cannot be sold, refinanced, or divided without everyone agreeing — or a judge deciding.

Once probate closes and the deed transfers to the siblings, the law recognizes them as tenants in common. Even a sibling who owns only 10% of the house has a 100% legal right to access and occupy it. The occupying sibling does not owe the others rent unless they change the locks or actively prevent entry — an ouster — and only then can the others sue for fair market rent. If the occupying sibling stops paying property taxes or the inherited mortgage, the home will eventually fall into foreclosure. If the house is still in probate, the picture is different and worse: the title remains in the name of the deceased or the estate, and traditional banks will not issue a standard mortgage or home equity line to someone who does not legally own the property yet. The only bridge is a probate advance, a short-term loan from a specialized lender, priced accordingly.

The law gives the family six paths, and only one of them is a buyout. One sibling buys the others out. The house is sold. The siblings agree to shared use. They go to mediation. The executor sells. Or someone files a partition action and a judge orders a sale, often at public auction. Heirs’ property law changes the order of those paths in many states: in Texas, for example, Chapter 23A gives the other siblings the first chance to buy out a sibling who wants out, at the appraised value, and the court favors dividing the property over selling it. Any joint owner can ask a court to divide or sell under Texas Property Code Section 23.001, but the buy-first right means the family gets the first shot at keeping the house. Harris County offers free mediation to qualifying residents. The executor, meanwhile, has a fiduciary duty to force a sale if the estate owes money — property taxes, Medicaid recovery, a mortgage — and lacks liquid cash, regardless of who lives there. An occupying sibling who is also the executor, living rent-free while taxes accrue, is breaching that duty.

The buyout itself is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is where families break. A buyout costs whatever the appraisal says, divided among the siblings: a house appraised at $400,000 with four equal siblings means the sibling who keeps the house owes the other three $300,000 in total. Every sibling must agree in writing to accept an independent appraiser’s figure — not a listing-site estimate, not the tax assessment. Once title has transferred, the keeping sibling usually borrows against the equity to fund the payout, and traditional banks cap cash-out refinances and home equity lines at about 80% of value. The tax side is the one piece of good news: inherited property generally takes its date-of-death value as its basis under IRS Publication 551, so a house bought for $100,000 and worth $800,000 at death carries an $800,000 basis, and a sale at $800,000 produces no taxable gain. The bad news hides in the property tax bill: in California, Proposition 19 means inheriting a home can trigger a reassessment, and the taxes can double or triple overnight.

The honest version is that a buyout is not always the cleanest answer, even though it usually is. An appraisal can be disputed for months. The keeping sibling may not be able to borrow $300,000 at any rate. A partition sale at auction often yields less than a market sale, and the legal fees come out of everyone’s share. Mediation is cheaper than a partition action and preserves more of the family than a courtroom does, but it only works if everyone shows up willing to agree. Sometimes the sale is the honest answer, and the sibling who wants to keep the house is the one who has to hear it.

That is why the appraisal is the hinge of the whole transaction. Get a date-of-death appraisal from a licensed appraiser in the first thirty days, before positions harden and before the tax record is set. Everything downstream — the buyout price, the financing, the tax basis, the partition value if it comes to that — runs off that number. The families that settle are the ones that agree on a number early. The families that end up in court are the ones that argued about the number for a year.
