The Buyout Loan: How the Keeping Heir Actually Pays the Others

Three siblings, one house, and a $400,000 gap. The loan that closes it is not the one most families reach for first — and the cheapest route depends on a rule most lenders never explain.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The house appraised at $600,000. The mortgage was paid off years ago. Three siblings inherited it equally, which meant the sister who wanted to keep it owed her two brothers $200,000 each — $400,000 in total, due at a closing table where nobody had $400,000. This is the ordinary shape of a probate buyout, and it is the point where most families discover that the hard part was never agreeing on the price. It is paying for it.

The most common answer is a mortgage, but not the kind most people picture. A cash-out refinance is the route families reach for first, and it is usually the wrong one. A standard cash-out refinance on a primary residence is capped at 80 percent of the home’s value, and it carries a six-month waiting period after taking title. The buyout loan is a different product with a different name: Fannie Mae treats paying out a co-owner under a written agreement as a limited cash-out refinance, and that classification changes everything. The loan-to-value limit rises to 95 percent on a home the buyer will live in, the standard 12-month seasoning requirement on any existing mortgage is waived when the proceeds buy out a co-owner, and the six-month wait after taking title does not apply to inherited property. Freddie Mac reached the same place from the other direction: since November 4, 2026, it calls the transaction an equity buyout refinance and classifies it as a no-cash-out refinance, eligible for the same loan-to-value limits as any no-cash-out refinance. The conditions are strict — a written agreement signed by every owner, no cash to the sibling keeping the house, the buyer qualifying alone on income and credit — but the math is what matters. On a $600,000 house with no mortgage, the keeping heir can borrow up to $570,000 at 95 percent, which covers the $400,000 owed to the siblings and leaves room for closing costs. The same house through a standard cash-out refinance tops out at $480,000.

The route only works if the legal groundwork is already done, and this is where buyouts stall. A sibling usually cannot get a mortgage while the home is still titled in the estate’s name, because the lender has no borrower to lend to. The right approach, in the words of one mortgage guide, is to treat the buyout as a formal sale, with a contract in place and the refinance, payoff, and transfer all happening together at closing. The buyout agreement is the document that makes it possible: it states the appraised value, each heir’s share, the payment amount, and a commitment from the departing siblings to sign a deed transferring their interest once they receive funds. Lenders require it because it tells them exactly how much money needs to flow out of the loan proceeds. Before any lender commits, every heir needs to agree on what the property is worth, and that means a formal appraisal from a licensed, independent appraiser. Online estimates and tax assessor values are starting points at best; relying on them almost guarantees a dispute. Once the appraisal is in hand, the arithmetic is simple. Subtract any outstanding mortgages or estate debts, divide the remaining equity by ownership share, and the number the keeping heir must finance is exact. Three equal heirs on a $600,000 house with no debt means two $200,000 shares. On a house with a $150,000 mortgage, the net equity is $450,000 and each share is $150,000 — but the keeping heir must also pay off the old loan, so the new mortgage has to cover $450,000, not $300,000.

There are three other routes, and each fits a different family. The second is buying from the estate: if the house is still in the estate, the sibling keeping it can buy it from the executor in what amounts to a family sale, and the siblings’ shares can become the down payment through a gift of equity — the price is set at full value, and the others give up part of what they would have received. In Pennsylvania this route carries a tax cost the others may not, because the state taxes transfers to siblings. The third route is keeping the parent’s mortgage. Federal law, the Garn–St. Germain Act, bars a lender from calling a home loan due because of a transfer to a relative resulting from the borrower’s death, and federal servicing rules require the servicer to recognize a confirmed heir as a successor. If the house still carries the parent’s loan at a low rate, keeping it can be the cheapest answer — and the siblings are then paid with a second loan, a home equity line, or a fixed home equity loan behind the existing first mortgage. The fourth route is the bridge: a short-term estate loan or probate loan, built for the unusual circumstance where title is still in a deceased person’s name and multiple heirs hold fractional interests. It is faster than a conventional mortgage and far more expensive, which is why it works best as a bridge to the permanent loan rather than as the loan itself.

Here is the counterargument, and it is the one that saves families from the most common mistake. The cheapest loan is not always the right loan, and the highest loan-to-value limit is not a gift. A buyout refinance at 95 percent leaves the keeping heir with almost no equity cushion: on a $600,000 house, $570,000 of debt means a $30,000 cushion, and a new roof or a lost job erases it in a season. The keeping heir must also qualify alone, on their own income and credit score, and many cannot — which is why the estate-sale route, the gift of equity, or an installment note from the siblings sometimes wins even though it looks worse on paper. An installment note, where the departing siblings are paid over years rather than at closing, costs no underwriting at all and keeps the family out of a bank’s timeline entirely. It requires something no lender can supply, which is trust that the payments will keep coming. And there is a quieter risk in the high-leverage route: the buyout refinance treats the transaction as a purchase, which means the keeping heir’s basis in the house going forward includes the amount paid for the siblings’ shares — a real tax benefit later — but only if the price was set by a real appraisal. A family that agrees on a low number to keep the loan small has also agreed on a low basis, and the tax bill arrives years later when the house is sold.

The sequence matters more than the rate. Settle the price first, with a licensed appraisal that every heir accepts. Put the buyout agreement in writing, with the price, the timeline, and the deed transfer spelled out. Then choose the loan — and choose it by asking which route the family can actually qualify for, not which one has the lowest advertised rate. A buyout refinance at 95 percent is the cheapest answer for a keeping heir with strong income and credit. A purchase from the estate with a gift of equity is the answer when the house is still in probate and the siblings can afford patience. Keeping the parent’s mortgage is the answer when the rate on it is one the family will never see again. The families who get this wrong are the ones who start with the loan and work backward to the price. The ones who get it right start with the appraisal, sign the agreement, and let the loan follow. The house is the same either way. The difference is whether the keeping heir owns it, or the loan does.
