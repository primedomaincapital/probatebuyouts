How Long a Probate Buyout Takes, Step by Step

The national average is twenty months. The buyout itself takes eight weeks. The gap between those two numbers is where most family buyouts stall — and it is almost entirely predictable.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

Ask a family how long a probate buyout takes and the answer is usually a shrug followed by a number that is wrong in the optimistic direction. The most careful study of the question, Trust & Will’s 2024 State of Probate Study, surveyed 1,000 Americans and found the national average is about twenty months from filing to final distribution — and only 2 percent of respondents guessed it correctly. The majority guessed far shorter, or admitted they had no idea. The buyout itself, once every heir has agreed on terms, typically takes two to four months, according to the probate guides that track these transactions. The difference between twenty months and eight weeks is not a rounding error. It is the whole story of a probate buyout, and it is almost entirely predictable once you know which clock is running.

The first clock is probate itself, and it starts the day the petition is filed. A simple estate with a valid will, a competent executor, and no disputes typically closes in six to twelve months. Add real property, multiple heirs, or tax filings and the range stretches to twelve to twenty-four months. Add a contest — a will challenge, a fight over the executor, a dispute about who owns what — and the range becomes two to five years. The single biggest reason probate is never a matter of weeks is the mandatory creditor-claim period every estate must observe: three to six months in most states, during which the estate cannot make final distributions. Florida gives creditors three months; New York gives them seven. That four-month swing is set before any estate can safely close, and no amount of family harmony shortens it. Real estate alone adds an estimated four to eight months to the timeline, because a house has to be appraised, maintained, insured, and eventually transferred or sold — and each of those steps waits on the one before it.

The second clock is the state, and it matters more than most families expect. The same estate can close in six months in Texas, where independent administration lets the executor work with minimal court supervision, or drag past twenty-four months in Los Angeles County, where supervised probate and crowded dockets stretch every hearing. California’s courts say a formal probate there usually takes nine to eighteen months. New York runs seven to twelve months for a simple estate and twelve to eighteen for a typical one. Florida runs six to nine months for a simple estate and nine to fifteen for a typical one. The fastest lane of all is the small-estate shortcut, available in most states for estates below a dollar threshold: a small-estate affidavit can close an estate in thirty to ninety days. The thresholds move regularly — California’s is $184,500 for 2026, and $208,850 for deaths on or after April 1, 2025; Florida raised its summary administration limit from $75,000 to $150,000 effective July 1, 2026. A family that qualifies for the shortcut has already won the timeline. A family that does not has to run the full course.

Before any heir can be paid, the executor has a job that has nothing to do with the family argument and everything to do with the court. The estate must be inventoried — every account, every debt, every piece of property listed and valued. Creditors must be notified and paid. Tax returns must be filed, including a final income tax return for the deceased and, where the estate earns income during administration, a return for the estate itself. Only then can the executor file a final accounting and ask the court to close the estate and distribute what remains. This is where the twenty-month average comes from: not from any single step, but from the sequence of them, each waiting on the one before. A buyout that tries to jump the queue — paying the departing siblings before the creditors are settled — risks exactly the outcome the creditor period exists to prevent: a late claim that lands on the keeping heir personally, because the estate has already distributed its assets. The patient move is to let the executor finish the inventory and the creditor notice before the buyout agreement is even drafted. The impatient move is to sign the agreement first and discover, six months later, that the estate owed a hospital bill nobody had mentioned.

The third clock is the buyout itself, and it runs in six steps. First, the appraisal: one to four weeks for a licensed appraiser to value the house and produce a report every heir accepts. Second, the buyout agreement: the written document stating the price, each heir’s share, the payment timeline, and the deed transfer — a week to a month, depending on how much the family argues about it. Third, the loan: a buyout refinance takes thirty to sixty days from application to closing, assuming the keeping heir qualifies and the title is clear. Fourth, the court’s blessing, where one is required: some states require court approval before an estate asset can be sold to a family member, and a hearing date can add two to eight weeks. Fifth, the closing: the refinance funds, the departing siblings are paid, the deed transfers, and the keeping heir becomes the sole owner. Sixth, the tax paperwork: the appraisal, the agreement, and the closing statement filed and kept, because the basis figures they establish will matter years from now. None of these steps is slow on its own. The delay comes from running them in sequence when they could have run in parallel — the appraisal ordered in month one instead of month nine, the loan application started while probate is still open rather than after it closes.

Here is the counterargument, and it is worth hearing before anyone tries to hurry. Speed is not always the goal, and the slow lane sometimes protects the buyer. Rushing a buyout before the creditor period closes can leave the keeping heir personally exposed if a late claim surfaces — the estate’s debts do not disappear because the family is in a hurry. A buyout that closes while probate is still open also risks a challenge from a creditor or a disinherited relative who argues the price was too low, and a court that agrees can unwind the whole transaction. The families who finish fastest are often the ones who spent the longest on the two steps that look like delay: the appraisal and the agreement. A price every heir accepts and a document every heir signs are what let the loan close in weeks instead of months. Skip them and the buyout does not get faster. It gets contested, and contested estates run two to five years.

The practical math is simple enough to do on the back of an envelope. Take the probate timeline for your state and your estate’s complexity — six months in Texas, twelve in Florida, eighteen in California — and add the buyout’s own two to four months, then add a month for the things nobody plans for. A straightforward estate in Texas closes its buyout in about nine months. The same estate in California takes closer to twenty. If the estate qualifies for a small-estate affidavit, the whole thing can be done in ninety days. The families who are surprised by the timeline are the ones who counted only the buyout and forgot the probate. The ones who are not surprised counted both clocks from the start, ordered the appraisal in the first month, and let the loan application run while the court did its work. The house does not get cheaper while everyone waits. The carrying costs — taxes, insurance, maintenance — run every month the estate stays open. The fastest buyout is not the one that starts earliest. It is the one that never has to wait for a step that could have been done months ago.
