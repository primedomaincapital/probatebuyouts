The Tax Bill on a Probate Buyout: Basis, Capital Gains, and the Step-Up

A house bought for $90,000 in 1985 and worth $640,000 at death carries a tax bill of nearly zero — if the paperwork is right. Get the appraisal wrong and the same house costs a sibling $40,000.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

A father bought a house in 1985 for $90,000. He died in 2026, when it was worth $640,000. Three children inherited it equally. One wanted to keep it; the other two wanted their shares. The keeping child paid each sibling $213,333 — a third of $640,000 — and the siblings who sold paid almost nothing in tax. The reason is the single most valuable sentence in the tax code for inheriting families: under Internal Revenue Code Section 1014, the cost basis of inherited property resets to its fair market value on the date of the previous owner’s death. The $550,000 of appreciation that accumulated over forty-one years simply disappears for capital gains purposes. The siblings were taxed, if at all, only on the difference between what they received and their share of the stepped-up basis — and because the buyout price was set at the date-of-death value, that difference was nearly zero.

The mechanics are straightforward once the rule is clear. Each heir who co-inherits a property gets their own proportional share of the stepped-up basis: three siblings inheriting a $640,000 house equally each hold a basis of about $213,333, not a third of what the parent originally paid. Inheriting is not itself a taxable event — the IRS does not tax anyone simply for receiving a house — and any gain is measured only from the stepped-up basis when the property is later sold. Inherited property is also automatically treated as long-term, no matter how briefly the heir held it: sell the day after inheriting and the gain still qualifies for the preferential long-term rates of 0, 15, or 20 percent, depending on taxable income. Even when the gain is zero, the sale must be reported on Form 8949 and Schedule D, with the basis documented as the fair market value at the date of death. The worked example shows why the appraisal matters more than anything else in the transaction. Without a qualified appraisal dated at or near the death, the basis figure is a guess — and guides warn that the IRS may default the basis to the original purchase price, which on our example would turn a zero gain into a $183,333 gain per sibling. At a 15 percent rate, that is more than $27,000 of tax on money nobody actually made.

The date-of-death value is the default, but it is not the only date the law allows. The executor can elect the alternate valuation date — six months after the death — and use the property’s value on that date instead, but only if the election lowers both the estate’s value and the estate tax, and only if it applies to every asset in the estate at once. For a family whose estate owes no federal estate tax, which is nearly every family under the $15,000,000 exemption, the election is rarely worth making: a lower valuation date means a lower basis, and a lower basis means a larger gain when the house is eventually sold. The election exists for estates that owe tax, not for families trying to save it. The practical point is narrower and more useful: the appraisal must be dated at or near the death, and it must be done by a qualified appraiser whose report states the fair market value on that specific date. A broker’s price opinion, a tax assessor’s value, or a website estimate is not a qualified appraisal, and the IRS has no reason to accept one as the basis figure. The appraisal is a few hundred dollars. The tax it protects against is measured in tens of thousands.

The keeping heir’s side of the ledger is where the rule does its quietest work. When the keeping child pays $213,333 to each sibling, that money becomes part of their basis in the house going forward: their own stepped-up share of $213,333 plus the $426,666 paid for the other two shares, for a total basis of $640,000 — the full value of the house. If the house is later sold for $700,000, the taxable gain is $60,000, not $610,000. If the keeping heir moves into the house and lives there as a primary residence for two of the next five years, the Section 121 exclusion can shelter up to $250,000 of gain — $500,000 for a married couple — on a later sale, under IRS Publication 523. The federal estate tax, for almost every family, does not enter the picture at all: the 2026 federal exemption is $15,000,000 per individual, so it affects almost no families. The tax that does reach ordinary families is the state one. Pennsylvania, for example, taxes transfers to siblings at 12 percent — a rate that applies to the inheritance itself, not the buyout, and one that a family in Pennsylvania discovers only when the bill arrives.

The Section 121 exclusion comes with a clock the keeping heir should know about before buying out the siblings. The exclusion shelters up to $250,000 of gain — $500,000 for a married couple — but only if the owner lived in the house as a primary residence for two of the five years before the sale. A keeping heir who buys out two siblings for $426,666, holds a total basis of $640,000, and sells the house a year later for $700,000 owes tax on the $60,000 gain, because they have not lived in it long enough to claim the exclusion. The same heir who lives in it for two years and then sells owes nothing on that gain. The difference is not a loophole. It is the rule working as written: the exclusion rewards the person who made the house a home, not the person who held it as an investment. A family that plans the buyout should plan the next two years at the same time, because the tax result of the eventual sale is decided by where the keeping heir sleeps, not by what the siblings agreed.

Here is the counterargument, and it is the one that catches the families who think the step-up makes everything free. The step-up covers appreciation before death. It does not cover appreciation after death, and a buyout priced above the date-of-death value creates a gain for the selling siblings on the difference. A house appraised at $640,000 at death and bought out at $700,000 a year later leaves each selling sibling with a gain of about $20,000 — small, but real, and taxable at long-term rates. The step-up also does nothing for the sibling who inherits a house held in joint tenancy with right of survivorship: the step-up applies only to the deceased’s portion, not the whole property. And the most common way families lose the benefit is the most ordinary one — they never get the appraisal. A family that agrees on a price by looking at a real estate website has agreed on a number the IRS has no reason to accept, and the sibling who sells at that price has no document to defend the basis figure if the return is examined. The appraisal is not a formality. It is the document the entire tax result rests on, and it costs a few hundred dollars against a tax bill that can run to tens of thousands.

The practical sequence is short. Get a qualified appraisal dated at or near the death, before anyone agrees on a buyout price. Set the buyout price from that appraisal, so the selling siblings’ gain is near zero and the keeping heir’s basis is the full value of the house. Put the price in the buyout agreement, in writing, with every heir’s signature. Report the sale on Form 8949 and Schedule D even when the gain is zero, and keep the appraisal, the agreement, and the closing statement indefinitely — the IRS expects the basis figure to be documented, and the document that proves it is the one most families throw away. The step-up is the most generous rule in the tax code for inheriting families, and it asks for almost nothing in return: a fair market value, measured once, on the day that matters, by someone qualified to measure it. Get that number right and the $550,000 of appreciation in our example vanishes. Get it wrong, or never get it at all, and the family pays tax on money their parent already earned — forty years after the fact, and entirely avoidably.
